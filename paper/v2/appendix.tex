\section{Protocols, predictions and deviations}\label{app:protocols}
Three protocols were written, according to our records, before their data existed, but they were committed to version control and hashed only after the data for D1, D1R and the H100 arm had been collected (same evening, 2026-09-25), so no independent timestamp shows the order. We therefore call their predictions planned, not pre-registered. Their timing rests on the protocol files' own statements and the run logs. The protocols are: the laptop experiment D1 (\path{research/experiments/dvfs-d1/protocol.md}), the RTX 4090 pod replication D1R (\path{dvfs-d1r/}) and the H100 arm (\path{dvfs-h100/}). A protocol for an API and batch-size follow-up (D2, \path{dvfs-d2/}) was written but not run and is not part of this paper. (An early commit message calls these three protocols preregistered; they were not.) The locked-clock experiment E-LOCK (\path{research/experiments/dvfs-lock/}) ran on the laptop only. GitHub's server-side push record, which an author cannot backdate, shows its protocol pushed at 05:09:15~UTC on 2026-10-19; our runner logged the first run starting at 05:20:13. Its analysis script was written after the data. E-SPLIT (\path{research/experiments/dvfs-split/}), which crosses the SM and memory locks, had its protocol pushed at 07:25:49~UTC on 2026-10-25, its analysis script at 07:33:29 and its amendment at 07:37:55; the first valid run started at 07:38:32. Its predictions were written after seeing E-LOCK's medians, as the protocol states. Run start times come from our own runner and are not externally timestamped. Neither protocol was filed with an independent registry such as OSF, so we call their predictions committed in advance rather than pre-registered in the registry sense. One exploratory pilot preceded them (\path{research/data/raw/2026-09-25-dvfs-pilot/}) and is used only to motivate the design. D1 predicted, for batch-1 queries:
P1, the heater lowers light-path end-to-end P50 by at least 10\% (ratio $<0.9$, interval excluding 1) at $E\le 3{,}000$;
P2, the heater does not speed up the heavy path (ratio $\ge 0.95$);
P3, the light path's ratio is below the heavy path's;
P4, the heater lowers light-path device time while host row materialization stays within $\pm10\%$.
The same rules were applied unchanged to every host.

P1 fails everywhere at the block level; the pilot's $1.9\times$ end-to-end speed-up on the laptop did not replicate reliably across blocks. P2 and the device-time half of P3 hold in every cell. P4 fails where the heater also slowed host-side work. On the H100 pod, host row materialization became 40--48\% slower with the heater running, which shows that the heater process took host CPU time as well as GPU time. The end-to-end slowdowns on that host therefore say little about the power-state account; they show that our heater is not a clean GPU-only perturbation.

\paragraph{Deviations.} (i) The clock summary in the planned analysis script took the median over the whole process, including data loading; we added a GPU-active median (utilization $>0$) before inspecting any cross-host comparison and report both in the released CSVs. (ii) Two pod setup attempts failed because of script bugs (a missing dataset copy, a mis-specified pod) and were re-run before any cell completed; no completed cell was discarded. (iii) The pod first assigned to block~3 ran host-side benchmark work roughly $10\times$ slower than the others (about $14\times$ on a single-threaded Python test). We re-ran block~3 from scratch on a new pod (Ryzen~9 7950X); by our records the decision was made on host speed before inspecting its results, but 14 of its cells had already completed. They are retained (\path{d1r-b3-partial.tar.gz}) and excluded from the tables. Keeping them would have widened the pods' spread, not narrowed it: the slow pod's light kernel took 1.084\,ms at $E=10{,}000$, against 0.128--0.589\,ms on the other pods. A stray setup process ran for about 30\,s on the block~4 pod during three cells; dropping those cells changes the affected light-path ratio from 0.967 to 0.966. (iv) The cross-host table (Table~\ref{tab:crosshost}) and the device-time framing were not planned; they were suggested by the retained September~24 archive and are therefore partly exploratory, although the pods and H100 supplied new data for them.

\paragraph{E-LOCK predictions.} K1 (light max/min $\le 1.15$ locked high), K3 (locked-low light time within 25\% of D1's slow mode at $E=10{,}000$), K4 (no heater effect locked high) and K5 (heavy within 3\% of unlocked) held in every tested cell. K2 (locked-high light time within 15\% of the best unlocked) failed: locked-high light time was 32--50\% \emph{lower} than the best unlocked median. No run was excluded and the run followed the protocol. Six heater-on, locked-high light runs held memory at 7,001 rather than the requested 8,001\,MHz although the lock command succeeded; the exclusion rule covered only the SM clock, and their times match the other locked-high runs, so K4 is unaffected. Analysis: \path{research/scripts/analyze_dvfs_lock.py}.

\paragraph{E-SPLIT predictions and deviations.} S0 (re-run E-LOCK corners within 10\% of E-LOCK), S1 (light max/min $\le 1.15$ under every lock), S2 (selection memory share $m<0.3$) and S3 (scoring $m<0.5$) held in every testable cell; S3's margin was small for light scoring at $E=10{,}000$ ($m=0.48$). One amendment was made before any valid data: the registered binary had been built without the \texttt{gpu-wgpu} feature, so the first attempt failed every process at startup with no measurements. It was rebuilt from the same commit with the feature, the new hash was committed as \path{amendment-1.md}, and the failed attempt is archived unanalyzed. Five light-path runs locked at SM 585\,MHz and memory 8,001\,MHz ran with memory at 7,001\,MHz and were excluded under the registered 5\% rule, leaving $n=1$ at $E=1{,}000$ and $n=4$ at $E=3{,}000$ for that condition. Analysis: \path{research/scripts/analyze_dvfs_split.py}.

\section{CPU/GPU crossover}\label{app:crossover}
Table~\ref{tab:crossover} gives, per GPU, $E$ and arm, the median over blocks of each block's end-to-end P50. The CPU's cost per eligible row is 83--103\,ns on the laptop and 44--80\,ns on the pods at $E\le 3{,}000$, then 306--333\,ns and 149--164\,ns at $E\ge 10{,}000$. The jump coincides with the CPU scan's switch from certified FP32 to FP64 accumulation above $E=4{,}096$ (Section~\ref{sec:system}), so the crossover between $E=3{,}000$ and $10{,}000$ is partly a property of our CPU kernel. Extrapolating the FP32 rate would put the laptop's CPU ahead of the light path at $E=10{,}000$ and the pods' at parity; we did not measure this. The H100 and host A had no CPU arm.

\section{The held-out router failure that motivated this study}\label{app:router}
The system's earlier research campaign (preserved unchanged in \path{paper/paper.tex}) selected a scalar routing rule, GPU if $E\cdot D\cdot B\ge 10^6$, with zero errors on 31 development CPU/GPU pairs. On a held-out suite of 16 workloads on an RTX 4050 laptop it chose the slower route in 8, with median regret 0.4\% and maximum regret 235.7\%. Each route was measured in a single process with five in-process runs. An audit of that archive found that the automatic arm differed from the forced arm of the same backend by more than 25\% in 9 of 16 cells, and that two of the eight ``wrong'' labels were within 3.2\% regret. In light of Section~\ref{sec:repro}, labels near parity in such a suite partly measure the GPU's power state during each process, and the per-workload count is not a stable statistic. Whether the 235.7\% maximum regret ($3.36\times$) reflects the rule or the GPU's state cannot be settled from that archive: the suite ran on the same laptop whose light kernel later varied up to $4.2\times$ between processes, and kernel-time spread is not the same quantity as end-to-end route regret. The earlier manuscript also documents representation, preparation and harness findings that are not repeated here.

\section{Cost and resources}
New cloud runs used five RTX 4090 pods (one block each), one H100 SXM pod and a 4-minute H100 probe, at \$0.74 and \$3.49 per hour. The operator's ledger totals about US\$6 (\path{research/experiments/dvfs-d1r/campaign-log.md}). All pods were deleted after their data were downloaded and hash-checked. A container-level Vulkan fix (pointing the loader at \texttt{libEGL\_nvidia.so.0} rather than the toolkit's GLX ICD) was required on every pod and is documented in the log; it may explain an earlier ``no Vulkan'' failure on an A6000.

The heavy kernel reproduces within 1\% between processes on each single machine (the laptop, 4090 host A and the H100), and within 7\% across the five pod hosts, which span two driver branches (570 and 580). \section{Which power state each path gets}\label{sec:states}
Table~\ref{tab:states} reports the median SM and memory clocks while the GPU was active (samples with non-zero utilization) for each path. \paragraph{Device time: the planned interaction appears on every GPU.} Without the heater, the light kernel runs a median 1.15--1.59$\times$ slower than its best, with a tail reaching $3.3\times$. With the heater, its median is 1.00--1.07; runs more than $1.5\times$ slow disappear on the laptop and the H100 and fall from 28\% to 16\% on the 4090 pods. The heavy kernel has no tail to remove. With the heater it becomes uniformly slower on the laptop ($1.19\times$) and the H100 ($1.36\times$), the cost of sharing the GPU, and stays unchanged on the 4090 pods. Paired ratios with block-bootstrap intervals are in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:heater}.

\paragraph{End to end: small and host-dependent.} The heater also competes for GPU submission and readback, and on some hosts for the CPU: on the H100 pod it slowed host row materialization by 40--48\%. End to end, the light path's median call latency with the heater was 0.94--0.96 of its heater-off value on the 4090 pods at $E=3{,}000$--$10{,}000$ (1.07 at 30{,}000) and 0.95--0.97 on the laptop at $E\ge 3{,}000$, with intervals that mostly include 1. On the H100 it was 1.10--1.24. Heavy calls were 6--9\% slower on the consumer GPUs and 25--27\% slower on the H100. Under the same added load the light path fared better than the heavy path in 10 of 13 cells, but whether it gained or lost overall depends on how much contention the host adds. The $1.9\times$ end-to-end speed-up in our laptop pilot did not replicate reliably across blocks.

\paragraph{Scorecard against the planned predictions (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:scorecard}).}
\begin{itemize}
\item P1 predicted a light-path end-to-end speed-up of at least 10\% at $E\le 3{,}000$. It \emph{failed} on every GPU.
\item P2 predicted no heavy-path speed-up. It held in every cell.
\item P3 predicted that the light path's ratio would fall below the heavy path's. It held in every cell for device time and in 10 of 13 cells end to end.
\item P4 predicted that light device time falls while host materialization stays within 10\%. It held in 6 of 13 cells and failed where the heater also slowed the host.
\end{itemize}
The device-level predictions held. P1, the end-to-end prediction and the one that mattered most for an application, did not.

\section{Locking the clock}\label{sec:lock}
On the laptop we ran the direct test. Each condition ran in a fresh process with the driver's default clocks (U), with SM and memory locked at the heavy path's boost (2,670 and 8,001\,MHz; Lhi), or locked at the light path's low state (585 and 810\,MHz; Llo). The heater was crossed with U and Lhi. The design was 6 cells $\times$ 5 conditions $\times$ 5 blocks $=150$ processes, with its protocol committed and hashed before the first run (Appendix~\ref{app:protocols}). No run was excluded, the observed SM clock matched the lock in every locked run, and the GPU never exceeded 50\,\textdegree C.

\paragraph{The variation disappears at fixed clocks.} Unlocked, the light kernel's device time varied by $2.84\times$, $2.11\times$ and $1.51\times$ across blocks at $E=1{,}000$, $3{,}000$ and $10{,}000$. Locked at boost, it varied by $1.00\times$ in all three cells (prediction K1, $\le 1.15$). The heater had cut unlocked light time to $0.57$, $0.37$ and $0.40$ of its heater-off value; with the clock locked it changed nothing (ratio $1.00$ in every cell; K4). The heavy kernel was already stable and unchanged by the lock (Lhi/U $=1.000$, $1.000$, $1.002$; K5).

\paragraph{The low state alone reproduces the slow mode.} Locked at 585/810\,MHz, the light kernel at $E=10{,}000$ took 0.444\,ms, within 4\% of the 0.461\,ms that D1 measured on the same laptop without locking (K3). The same lock slowed the heavy kernel by $3.8\times$ (0.95 to 3.6\,ms), so the slow state is costly for both paths. The driver put the light path there and kept the heavy path out of it.

\paragraph{One prediction failed.} K2 predicted that locking high would match the best unlocked speed to within 15\%. Locked-high light time was 0.50--0.68 of the best unlocked median (0.63--0.81 of the best single unlocked run), outside that tolerance, so K2 fails. After the fact, we note that the lock beat every unlocked configuration, including unlocked with the heater. That suggests the driver's best unlocked state for light work is still below boost, but we did not predict it.

\paragraph{What this does and does not show.} On this laptop, fixing the clocks removes the light kernel's process-to-process variation and sets its level, so the clock the driver chooses accounts for the variation we measured. We could not repeat the test on the cloud hosts, so the cross-host spread there is attributed to power state by the heater and telemetry evidence only.

A follow-up on the same laptop, E-SPLIT, crossed them: SM at 2,670 or 585\,MHz, memory at 8,001 or 810\,MHz, both paths, the same three $E$, five blocks, 120 fresh processes. Its protocol and analysis script were committed and pushed before its data. The first attempt used a binary built without the GPU backend and failed at startup; the rebuild was recorded as an amendment before any valid run (Appendix~\ref{app:protocols}). Re-running E-LOCK's two corners reproduced its medians within 0.2\%. \paragraph{The slow mode is the SM clock.} For the selection kernel, which dominates device time and is the metric in every other table, lowering only the memory clock changed nothing at $E\le 3{,}000$ and slowed the light path by 10\% at $E=10{,}000$ ($m\le 0.07$ everywhere; prediction S2, $m<0.3$). Lowering only the SM clock reproduced nearly all of the slow mode. The scoring kernel was also SM-driven at $E\le 3{,}000$. At $E=10{,}000$ the memory clock did matter for scoring: lowering it alone doubled light-path scoring time (0.034 to 0.069\,ms; $m=0.48$, just inside the registered limit of 0.5) and raised heavy-path scoring by 44\%. There the two clocks do not act independently ($m+s=1.35$ for light scoring, where $s$ is the same share for the SM clock). The memory cut we saw in telemetry at $E=10{,}000$ therefore costs something in the scoring kernel, but the variation in Table~\ref{tab:crosshost} comes mainly from the SM clock. A memory-bound explanation of the slow mode does not hold at these sizes.

\section{Consequences}\label{sec:consequences}
\paragraph{Unlocked-clock benchmarks can understate the efficient implementation.} On the laptop, the light kernel at fixed boost ran 2.9--5.0$\times$ faster than with default clocks, so the unlocked number understates it. A benchmark that compares a work-efficient GPU kernel with a less efficient one at low load runs the efficient one at a lower clock. Here the light path won every cell regardless. But at $E=10{,}000$ its device-time advantage over the heavy path is $1.6\times$ on 4090 host A, $2.0\times$ on the laptop, $4.7\times$ on the 4090 pods and $4.8\times$ on the H100 (medians of three to five processes, from Table~\ref{tab:crosshost}), for identical code. On the laptop with both paths locked at boost it is $8.3\times$ (Table~\ref{tab:lock}). Optimizations that remove GPU work (tighter filtering, smaller kernels, early exit) can be measured at a discount the benchmark does not show, and the discount differs between machines.

\paragraph{Kernel-level results did not transfer between machines with the same GPU.} On RTX 4090s (host A and the five pods), light-kernel times for the same cell at $E=10{,}000$ ran from 0.128 to 0.589\,ms across processes, a $4.6\times$ span. Any threshold or cost model calibrated on one of them, for example a CPU/GPU router, partly encodes that host's power behavior. The study that preceded this one fitted a scalar CPU/GPU routing threshold with zero errors on 31 development pairs. On a held-out suite it chose the slower route in 8 of 16 workloads, several of them within the process-to-process variation measured here (Appendix~\ref{app:router}). In our data the heater never changed a CPU/GPU winner (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:crossover}). We therefore do not claim that power state moves this system's crossover, only that it moves the margins a router is calibrated on.

\paragraph{Isolated benchmarks may misstate co-located latency.} In deployment a retrieval kernel often shares the GPU with a model; our heater is a crude stand-in for that load. On an idle GPU the light path is exposed to deep power states; under load, to contention. The heavy path is exposed only to contention. Latency measured on an idle GPU is therefore a poor proxy for latency under co-location, and the error can differ between implementations. We did not test co-location with a real model.

\paragraph{What to report.} For latency-bound GPU work:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] Replicate across fresh processes and report the spread between them; repetitions inside one process share its power state.
\item[(ii)] Log clocks and performance state continuously, not just before and after a run.
\item[(iii)] Report device-timestamp kernel time alongside end-to-end latency, to separate power-state effects from host effects.
\item[(iv)] Lock clocks where possible, and report results at the locked clock alongside the default. Where clocks cannot be locked, add a load-raising control such as the heater, together with a heavy negative control.
\item[(v)] When comparing implementations, state whether they ran in the same power state.
\end{enumerate}

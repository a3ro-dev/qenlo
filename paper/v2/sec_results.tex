At the $E$ values in Table~\ref{tab:crosshost} the heavy kernel reproduces within 1\% between processes on each single machine (the laptop, 4090 host A and the H100), and within 7\% across the five pod hosts, which span two driver branches (570 and 580). The light kernel does not. Its spread between processes reaches $2.6\times$ on 4090 host A and $4.2\times$ on the laptop (both at $E=3{,}000$), and $2.1$--$4.6\times$ across the five 4090 pods ($4.3$--$4.6\times$ at $E\ge 3{,}000$). The per-process values look bimodal (Figure~\ref{fig:central}a), so a median of three to five processes mostly reports which mode most of them landed in. Host A's and the pods' ranges overlap at every $E$; we read the difference between their medians as a difference in how often each landed in the slow mode, not as a stable property of the host. The light path is faster than the heavy path in every cell.

The retained archive shows the same pattern within one host over time. Across the ten earlier identical blocks at $E=3{,}000$, the light path's selection time spans $2.44\times$ and correlates with the SM clock in an \texttt{nvidia-smi} snapshot taken just after each process ended (Spearman $\rho=-0.83$, exact two-sided $p=0.005$). That clock is a coarse, end-of-process proxy, the correlation rests largely on the two fast blocks, and the clock is partly endogenous (Section~\ref{sec:states}). The heavy kernels in the same archive vary by at most 0.01\% at $E\le 10{,}000$ and 0.7\% at $E=100{,}000$, where their clock was at boost, but by 7\% at $E=30{,}000$, where it was not.

\section{Which power state each path gets}\label{sec:states}
Table~\ref{tab:states} reports the median SM and memory clocks while the GPU was active (samples with non-zero utilization) for each path. On every GPU the heavy path holds boost SM clocks: 2,670--2,820\,MHz on the consumer GPUs and 1,980\,MHz on the H100. The light path runs lower. At $E=10{,}000$ the driver also cuts the memory clock to 810\,MHz on the laptop (from 8,201\,MHz; performance state P5) and on the 4090 pods (from 10,501\,MHz). (The heavy path's own memory clock sat at 7,001\,MHz in the laptop's $E=3{,}000$ cell.) Here both clocks fall together, so telemetry alone cannot say which of them slows the kernels; Section~\ref{sec:split} separates them. On the H100 the light path's SM clock falls from 1,605 to 645\,MHz between $E=1{,}000$ and $10{,}000$. On the laptop, sampled clocks predict the heater's effect on device time well. On the cloud hosts they do not: the pods' median light-path SM clock is 79--89\% of boost, which cannot by itself produce a $4.6\times$ spread, and on the H100 at $E=10{,}000$ the heater raised the median clock $3.1\times$ but sped the kernel only $1.23\times$. Telemetry at this resolution supports the power-state account on the laptop and is only consistent with it elsewhere.

The governor's inputs are undocumented, and we do not try to model them; we report what we observe. At default clocks the heavy path spends 52--84\% of each call inside its two kernels and always holds boost; the light path spends 13--43\% and does not (\path{research/scripts/busy_fraction.py}). Within the light path the clock \emph{falls} as $E$ grows on the laptop and the H100, even though per-call device work grows. That default-clock share is endogenous, because a lower clock lengthens the kernels that define it. At locked boost on the laptop (Tables~\ref{tab:lock} and~\ref{tab:split} over the call times of Appendix Table~\ref{tab:crossover}) the kernels fill 13.5\%, 10.4\% and 8.7\% of a call at $E=1{,}000$, $3{,}000$ and $10{,}000$, because host-side preparation of the $E$ ids grows faster than device work. A governor that reacts to GPU utilization would therefore lower the clock as $E$ grows, as we observe. We did not test this directly, for example by varying the host gap between queries at a fixed $E$. The NVIDIA policy is undocumented \citep{spaan2026kerneldvfs}, and our claims concern its observable consequences.

\section{Raising the load where clocks could not be locked}\label{sec:intervention}
Each experiment ran every condition (engine $\times$ $E$ $\times$ heater on/off) once per block, in shuffled order and fresh processes (Section~\ref{sec:setup}). Table~\ref{tab:tail} gives the most direct test. For every block and $E$, it divides each path's device time by the best value observed for that GPU and $E$ in either arm. \paragraph{Device time: the planned interaction appears on every GPU, clearly only on the laptop.} Without the heater, the light kernel runs a median 1.15--1.59$\times$ slower than its best, with a 90th percentile reaching $3.3\times$. With the heater, its median is 1.00--1.07. Runs more than $1.5\times$ slow fall from 13 of 25 to 0 on the laptop, from 7 to 4 of 25 on the 4090 pods and from 1 to 0 of 9 on the H100; only the laptop's change is more than a few runs. The heavy kernel has no tail to remove. With the heater it becomes slower on the laptop ($1.18$--$1.19\times$ at $E\le 10{,}000$, unchanged at $30{,}000$) and the H100 ($1.36\times$), the cost of sharing the GPU, and stays unchanged on the 4090 pods. Paired ratios with block-bootstrap intervals are in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:heater}.

\paragraph{End to end: small and host-dependent.} The heater also competes for GPU submission and readback, and on some hosts for the CPU: on the H100 pod it slowed host row materialization by 40--48\% at $E\le 3{,}000$. End to end, the light path's median call latency with the heater was 0.94--0.96 of its heater-off value on the 4090 pods at $E=3{,}000$--$10{,}000$ (1.07 at 30{,}000) and 0.95--0.97 on the laptop at $E\ge 3{,}000$, with intervals that mostly include 1. On the H100 it was 1.10--1.24. Heavy calls were 6--9\% slower on the consumer GPUs and 25--27\% slower on the H100. Under the same added load the light path fared better than the heavy path in 10 of 13 cells, but whether it gained or lost overall depends on how much contention the host adds. The $1.9\times$ end-to-end speed-up in our laptop pilot did not replicate reliably across blocks.

\paragraph{Scorecard against the planned predictions (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:scorecard}).}
\begin{itemize}
\item P1 predicted a light-path end-to-end speed-up of at least 10\% at $E\le 3{,}000$. It \emph{failed} on every GPU.
\item P2 predicted no heavy-path speed-up. It held in every cell.
\item P3 predicted that the light path's ratio would fall below the heavy path's. It held in every cell for device time (on the pods at $E=30{,}000$ the two ratios tie at two decimals) and in 10 of 13 cells end to end.
\item P4 predicted that light device time falls while host materialization stays within 10\%. It held in 6 of 13 cells and failed where the heater also slowed the host.
\end{itemize}
The device-level predictions held. P1, the end-to-end prediction and the one that mattered most for an application, did not.

\section{Locking the clock}\label{sec:lock}
On the laptop we ran the direct test. Each condition ran in a fresh process with the driver's default clocks (U), with SM and memory locked at boost (SM 2,670\,MHz, the heavy path's observed value; memory 8,001\,MHz, which \texttt{nvidia-smi} then reports as 8,201; Lhi), or locked at the light path's low state (585 and 810\,MHz; Llo). The heater was crossed with U and Lhi. The design was 6 cells $\times$ 5 conditions $\times$ 5 blocks $=150$ processes, with its protocol pushed to the public repository 11 minutes before the first run (Appendix~\ref{app:protocols}). No run was excluded, the sampled SM clock matched the lock in every locked run, and the GPU never exceeded 50\,\textdegree C. Six heater-on, locked-high light runs held memory at 7,001 rather than 8,001\,MHz; the protocol's exclusion rule covered only the SM clock, and their times equal those of the other locked-high runs.

\paragraph{The variation disappears at fixed clocks.} Unlocked, the light kernel's device time varied by $2.84\times$, $2.11\times$ and $1.51\times$ across blocks at $E=1{,}000$, $3{,}000$ and $10{,}000$. Locked at boost, it varied by $1.00\times$ in all three cells (prediction K1, $\le 1.15$). The heater had cut unlocked light time to $0.57$, $0.37$ and $0.40$ of its heater-off value; with the clock locked it changed nothing (ratio $1.00$ in every cell; K4). The heavy kernel was already stable and unchanged by the lock (Lhi/U $=1.000$, $1.000$, $1.002$; K5).

\paragraph{The low state alone reproduces the slow mode.} Locked at 585/810\,MHz, the light kernel at $E=10{,}000$ took 0.444\,ms, within 4\% of the 0.461\,ms that D1 measured on the same laptop without locking (K3, which was registered against D1). Against E-LOCK's own unlocked arm (0.567\,ms) the low lock is 22\% faster, so it reproduces the slow level rather than bounding it. A lock this low may itself put the GPU in an idle-like P-state \citep{adamek2021fftedge}, so Llo is a close match to the driver's slow mode, not necessarily the same state. The same lock slowed the heavy kernel by $3.8\times$ (0.95 to 3.6\,ms; Table~\ref{tab:split}, LL, reproduces it), so the slow state is costly for both paths. The driver put the light path there and kept the heavy path out of it.

\paragraph{One prediction failed.} K2 predicted that locking high would match the best unlocked speed to within 15\%. Locked-high light time was 0.50--0.68 of the best unlocked median (0.63--0.81 of the best single unlocked run), outside that tolerance, so K2 fails. After the fact, we note that the lock beat every unlocked configuration, including unlocked with the heater. That suggests the driver's best unlocked state for light work is still below boost, but we did not predict it.

\paragraph{The unlocked baseline drifted.} E-LOCK's unlocked light medians (0.069, 0.176 and 0.567\,ms) are 1.2--2.3$\times$ slower than D1's on the same laptop three and a half weeks earlier (0.030, 0.106 and 0.461\,ms; Table~\ref{tab:crosshost}). D1 saw no spread at $E=1{,}000$ and no heater effect there; E-LOCK saw $2.84\times$ and 0.57. We did not record what changed between the two dates (driver updates, Windows build, power settings). The unlocked state of this laptop is not stable over weeks, which is itself an argument for locking, and the locked medians are what we compare across experiments.

\paragraph{What this does and does not show.} On this laptop, fixing the clocks removes the light kernel's process-to-process variation and sets its level, so the clock the driver chooses accounts for the variation we measured. We could not repeat the test on the cloud hosts, so the cross-host spread there is attributed to power state by the heater and telemetry evidence only.

\paragraph{Which clock matters.}\label{sec:split} E-LOCK moved the SM and memory clocks together. A follow-up on the same laptop, E-SPLIT, crossed them: SM at 2,670 or 585\,MHz, memory at 8,001 or 810\,MHz, both paths, the same three $E$, five blocks, 120 fresh processes. Its protocol and analysis script were committed and pushed before its data. The first attempt used a binary built without the GPU backend and failed at startup; the rebuild was recorded as an amendment before any valid run (Appendix~\ref{app:protocols}). Re-running E-LOCK's two corners reproduced its medians within 0.2\%. Table~\ref{tab:split} reports the medians and, for each kernel, the memory share $m=\ln(t_{\mathrm{HL}}/t_{\mathrm{HH}})/\ln(t_{\mathrm{LL}}/t_{\mathrm{HH}})$, where $m=0$ means lowering only the memory clock costs nothing and $m=1$ means it accounts for the whole slow mode.

\paragraph{The slow mode is the SM clock.} For the selection kernel, which dominates device time and is the metric in every other table, lowering only the memory clock changed nothing at $E\le 3{,}000$ and slowed the light path by 10\% at $E=10{,}000$ ($m\le 0.07$ everywhere; prediction S2, $m<0.3$). Lowering only the SM clock reproduced nearly all of the slow mode. The scoring kernel was also SM-driven at $E\le 3{,}000$. At $E=10{,}000$ the memory clock did matter for scoring: lowering it alone doubled light-path scoring time (0.034 to 0.069\,ms; $m=0.48$, just inside the registered limit of 0.5) and raised heavy-path scoring by 44\%. There the two clocks do not act independently ($m+s=1.35$ for light scoring, where $s$ is the same share for the SM clock). The memory cut we saw in telemetry at $E=10{,}000$ therefore costs something in the scoring kernel, but the variation in Table~\ref{tab:crosshost} comes mainly from the SM clock. A memory-bound explanation of the slow mode does not hold at these sizes.

\section{Consequences}\label{sec:consequences}
\paragraph{On the laptop, unlocked timing understated the efficient kernel.} At fixed boost the light kernel ran 2.9--5.0$\times$ faster than E-LOCK's unlocked arm, 1.3--4.1$\times$ faster than D1's unlocked medians and 1.5--2.0$\times$ faster than the best unlocked median, with the heater (K2). How much the unlocked number understates it depends on which unlocked run is the reference, but every reference understates it. A benchmark that compares a work-efficient GPU kernel with a less efficient one at low load runs the efficient one at a lower clock. Here the light path won every cell regardless. But at $E=10{,}000$ its device-time advantage over the heavy path is $1.6\times$ on 4090 host A, $2.0\times$ on the laptop, $4.7\times$ on the 4090 pods and $4.8\times$ on the H100 (medians of three to five processes, from Table~\ref{tab:crosshost}), for identical code. On the laptop with both paths locked at boost it is $8.3\times$ (Table~\ref{tab:lock}). We expect, but did not test, that optimizations which remove GPU work (tighter filtering, smaller kernels, early exit) are measured at a similar discount, and that the discount differs between machines. Our heavy path is a naive comparator; a tuned selection kernel would load the GPU differently.

\paragraph{Kernel-level results did not transfer between machines with the same GPU.} On RTX 4090s (host A and the five pods), light-kernel times for the same cell at $E=10{,}000$ ran from 0.128 to 0.589\,ms across the five pods' processes, a $4.6\times$ span (host A's fall inside it). Any threshold or cost model calibrated on one of them, for example a CPU/GPU router, partly encodes that host's power behavior. The study that preceded this one fitted a scalar CPU/GPU routing threshold with zero errors on 31 development pairs. On a held-out suite it chose the slower route in 8 of 16 workloads, two of them within 3.2\% regret, well inside the process-to-process variation measured here (Appendix~\ref{app:router}). In our data the heater never changed a CPU/GPU winner (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:crossover}). We therefore do not claim that power state moves this system's crossover, only that it moves the margins a router is calibrated on.

\paragraph{Isolated benchmarks may misstate co-located latency.} In deployment a retrieval kernel often shares the GPU with a model; our heater is a crude stand-in for that load. Co-location is known to slow latency-critical GPU work through interference \citep{han2022reef,strati2024orion}; the opposite effect, a co-runner raising a shared clock, has been observed for MIG partitions \citep{patel2023cloudgpu}. On an idle GPU the light path is exposed to deep power states; under load, to contention. The heavy path is exposed only to contention. Latency measured on an idle GPU may therefore be a poor proxy for latency under co-location, and the error can differ between implementations. We did not test co-location with a real model.

\paragraph{What to report.} For latency-bound GPU work we would follow the rules below. Rules (i)--(iii) and the first half of (iv) restate existing guidance \citep{kalibera2013rigorous,hoefler2015scientific,nvidia_tensorrt_env,nvidia_nvbench,nvidia_ncu}; the load-raising control with a heavy negative control in (iv), and rule (v), are to our search new.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] Replicate across fresh processes and report the spread between them; repetitions inside one process share its power state.
\item[(ii)] Log clocks, performance state and clock-event reasons continuously, not just before and after a run, and record the driver's power-management mode.
\item[(iii)] Report device-timestamp kernel time alongside end-to-end latency, to separate power-state effects from host effects.
\item[(iv)] Lock clocks where possible, and report results at the locked clock alongside the default. Where clocks cannot be locked, add a load-raising control such as the heater, together with a heavy negative control.
\item[(v)] When comparing implementations, state whether they ran in the same power state.
\end{enumerate}
